\section{Simulation environment}

\subsection{Traffic simulator}

The simulation software chosen is the SUMO traffic simulator~\cite{AlvarezLopez2018}, which is a microscopic and continuous traffic simulator that allows for the simulation of multimodal networks. As a microscopic simulator, SUMO models the behaviour of individual vehicles and pedestrians, allowing for a detailed representation of traffic dynamics by the combination of a variety of specific vehicle and traffic models.

The choice for using SUMO as the simulation environment was mainly motivated by its usage on the reinforcement learning framework that this work builds upon and which is detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:reinforcement_learning_framework}; however, several other reasons support this selection.

Firstly, SUMO is open source software, which allows for a better understanding of its inner workings and provides the possibility to modify it freely to adapt it to our needs. This open source nature enables on one hand that the works of the research community can easily be incorporated to it, like advanced vehicle dynamics models or additional scenarios; while it also offers great flexibility for integration with other tools. The latter feature is particularly relevant, as SUMO offers a Python API, the \gls{traci}, which can be used to communicate with a running simulation process.

Besides its possibilites for integration, SUMO also offers many utilities designed to process simulation inputs and outputs, such as traffic demand or simulation logs. This allows for a more efficient development of the simulation scenarios and the analysis of the results.

Finally, SUMO is a well-established traffic simulator, which has been used in a wide range of research studies and applications, making it a reliable and trusted tool for traffic simulation. Its possibilites have made it the most popular choice in the \gls{rltsc} literature, as shown by the different literature reviews examined in this work, and its usage is increasing over the years. In 2021 Noaeen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Noaeen2022} found that 17 out of 27 surveyed papers relied on SUMO (a 63\% prevalence). Four years later, in 2025, the review of Michailidis \textit{et al.}~\cite{Michailidis2025} showed that the popularity of SUMO as a choice had increased to a 68\% prevalence, being used on 38 out of 56 works examined.

\medskip

\noindent The SUMO simulator is written in C++ and uses XML files as its inputs and outputs. As input for a simulation, it requires a network file, which describes the road or street network that defines the simulation environment, and a trip file, which contains traffic demand information over the network. Both of these files can be generated from real data from using different built-in tools, as will be explained in Section~\ref{sec:scenario_design}. The simulator can be run in command line mode, which allows for a faster execution of the simulation, or in GUI mode, which provides a graphical interface to visualize the simulation and its results.

\subsection{Pedestrian and cyclist models}

The model that SUMO uses for pedestrians by default is the \textit{striping model}, which models the interactions of pedestrians with vehicles and themselves. The model achieves high execution speed by only considering close-range interactions along piecewise linear paths. This is done by separating the available space into narrow stripes along the walking direction and only interacting with at most one other traffic object per stripe~\cite{AlvarezLopez2018}.

Pedestrians in SUMO can travel in any lane that allows them in its vehicle permissions attribute, but they also enjoy exclusive map features. Firstly, any lane only allowing pedestrians is considered as a sidewalk, which unlike other SUMO lanes is not bound by directionality, letting pedestrians inside travel freely in any direction. SUMO pedestrians also use crossings to cross lanes used by vehicles, which can be either controlled by traffic signals or uncontrolled. In the case of controlled crossings, pedestrians can only cross when the traffic signal is green for them, while in uncontrolled crossings they can cross at any time, with vehicles yielding to them. Finally, SUMO adds, inside each intersection, pedestrian-only areas that connect the sidewalks of the approach edges among themselves and with the pedestrian crossings in the junction. These are referred to as \texttt{walkingAreas}.

\medskip

\noindent Bicycles, in the other hand, lack a dedicated model. The options to simulate them are either to do so by treating them as smaller and slower cars —the current default—, or to behave as a fast pedestrian~\cite{Kaths2023}. While lacking a specific model for bicycles, their simulation in SUMO greatly benefits from its \textit{sublane model}, which allows for the longitudinal division of driving lanes into multiple sub-lanes using lateral dynamics, the width of which can be defined by the user~\cite{AlvarezLopez2018}. This permits to model common behaviours of cyclists, such as riding side by side on a same lane if it is wide enough.

In the rest of aspects, bicycles in SUMO under the default settings behave as small vehicles, bound to lanes that allow them in their vehicle permissions and only moving in a single direction, according to the lane on which they travel.

\medskip

\noindent While the default SUMO software does not provide any further models for accurate multimodal simulation at the time of writing, the research community has developed some integrations to bring more realistic models to the simulator.

In the case of pedestrians, SUMO can be coupled with the pedestrian-specific \gls{jupedsim} software, which uses several microscopic models to provides a more realistic simulation of pedestrian dynamics, especially when large crowds develop~\cite{Chraibi2026}. Among the models it uses, the chosen for the scenario is the collision-free speed model developed by Tordeux \textit{et al.}~\cite{Tordeux2015}.

For cyclists, the Realistic Bicycle Dynamics Model for SUMO was developed to simulate bicycles using the real-world data from the SimRa dataset~\cite{Ostendorf2025}. This model provides a more accurate representation of bicycle dynamics, including realistic acceleration and deceleration behaviour, as well a better simulation of the various types of cyclists.

\medskip

\noindent Unfortunately, while performing the experiments it was found that it was not possible to align the usage of these models with the reinforcement learning framework used in this work.

For the Realistic Bicycle Dynamics Model, it is built upon an outdated version of SUMO (1.8) and it was found that integrating it into the current was not a trivial task, as the modifications required to the simulator itself were well out of the scope of this thesis.

Regarding \gls{jupedsim}, it was discovered that its independent simulation of pedestrians does not accurately propagate pedestrian positions to \gls{traci} standard pedestrian commands. Despite extensive changes to the traffic signal code for finding alternative ways of reporting pedestrian positions, it was not possible to report the location of people afoot accurately on the vicinity of pedestrian crossings.

Having stumbled with the aforementioned challenges, it was considered that the default models in SUMO for pedestrians and cyclists had a reasonable level of detail for the purposes of this work despite their limitations, and thus they were used for the experiments. However, it is worth noting that the usage of more realistic models for these modes could be a valuable avenue for future research, as it could provide a more accurate representation of the behaviour and interactions of non-motorized modes in traffic scenarios.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning framework} \label{subsec:reinforcement_learning_framework}

This work has been developed on top of the T-REX \gls{rltsc} framework presented by Nguyen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Nguyen2025}, which is a Python-based framework that provides a set of tools and libraries for developing and testing \gls{rltsc} methods in combination with SUMO's \gls{traci} interface. It is derived from the RESCO framework released by Ault and Sharon~\cite{Ault2021}.

According to its developers, T-REX is designed primarily as an offline tool to support the training and evaluation of control policies, and \gls{rl}-based methods in particular, by providing realistic traffic dynamics. Its features also include the introduction of disruptions such as lane blockages, rerouting behaviour, and speed adaptation. T-REX is built in four main modules: \textit{Network environment}, \textit{Initializer}, \textit{Deployment} and \textit{RL inferface}. The \textit{Network environment} module initializes the SUMO scenario with the network layout, demand information, signal plans and vehicle attributes; the \textit{Initializer} and \textit{Deployment} modules are responsible for incident simulation and thus unused in the present work; and the \textit{RL interface} module provides an interface for integrating \gls{rl} agents with the simulation environment.

Further implementationdetails about the T-REX framework can be found in the original publication that described it~\cite{Nguyen2025}.

\medskip

\noindent In this work, we extended T-REX to systematically train and assess \gls{rltsc} methods in a multimodal setting. The changes made to the framework as part of the development of the proposed methods mainly concern parts of the \gls{rl} formulation and their implementation, as such, these modifications are presented in Section~\ref{sec:proposed_models}.

\subsection{Baseline TSC models} \label{subsec:baseline_models}
An assorted number of state-of-the-art \gls{rltsc} methods were included in the \gls{rl} framework, as well as several traditional traffic signal control methods. The former were used both as starting point for the development of the multimodal models, and as baselines for experiments to compare the performance of the proposed models against other \gls{rltsc} methods in a multimodal setting. The latter were included solely to provide benchmarks for the mentioned experiments.

While the initial choice of \gls{rltsc} and traditional methods was based on their integration in T-REX framework employed in this work, further reasons informed the selection of the models presented as baselines. The rationale explaining their initial inclusion in the RESCO and T-REX frameworks can be found in the relevant original publications, \cite{Ault2021} and \cite{Nguyen2025}, respectively.

Besides their diversity in learning strategies and coordination levels, and their validation in the literature —see \cite{Ault2019}, \cite{Ault2021}, \cite{Chen2020}, \cite{Ma2020} or \cite{Nguyen2025}—, these models can also provide valuable insights on \gls{rltsc} in a multimodal context. Bicycle traffic and especially pedestrian traffic requires a detection infrastructure much more complex than that used for motorized vehicles, and thus assessing methods with diverse inputs and reward functions can help to bridge the gap between simulation and real-world sensor setups for multimodal \gls{tsc}. The different speeds and behaviour of pedestrians and cyclists compared to cars can also have important implications on affairs such as phase length, signal coordination or state representation, which we deem as relevant topics for the development of \gls{rltsc} methods that account for all street users.

Furthermore, while the presented baseline methods have been proven in maps based on real-world networks, they have not been tested in scenarios with a complexity comparable to the ones used in this work, with multimodal traffic, compound intersections with special lanes and non-conventional traffic behaviours such as Copenhagen left turns. Hence, the performance of these methods can yield, too, acumen on the applicability of \gls{rltsc} methods to real-world street networks.

Finally, while the baseline methods used in this work were \textit{not} modified to account for multimodal traffic, they have not been modified \textit{either} to ignore it. As will be described as part of the proposed models' definition in further pages —see Section~\ref{sec:proposed_models}—, the T-REX observation functions that relay traffic information were extended to include pedestrians and cyclists; hence, using the same observation tensor, baseline methods can still receive bike traffic data —for SUMO treats both cars and bicycles as vehicles— and optimize including it, without architectural changes. However, the lack of modifications also results on the methods aggregating bicycle and car traffic together, thus treating them equivalently. In this way, the possibility of integrating bicycle traffic into existing \gls{rltsc} architectures can also be studied, as well as the behaviour of mixed bicycle and car traffic in a \gls{rltsc} context.

\noindent \textbf{\Gls{rltsc} methods}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Independent Deep Q-Network (IDQN)~\cite{Ault2019}: a single-agent \gls{rltsc} method that uses a \gls{dqn} to learn the optimal policy for each intersection independently. It uses a state representation based on the number of vehicles waiting at each lane, and a reward function based on the total waiting time of vehicles, with lane-level traffic state features aggregated with convolutional layers. Its architecture is explained further in section tal.
    \item Independent Proximal Policy Optimization (IPPO)~\cite{Ault2019}: a single-agent \gls{rltsc} method that uses a policy gradient method (PPO) to learn the optimal policy for each intersection independently. It uses the same rewards and state representation as IDQN, which enables a good comparison between value-based and policy gradient methods in a multimodal context.
    \item MPLight~\cite{Chen2020}: a decentralized \gls{rltsc} method that coordinates the traffic signal control of multiple intersections by adding queue pressure into each agent's state representation, enabling it to partially observe neighbouring intersections. With this information, Q-values are estimated for each phase, and their selection done in a phase-competition basis. Its architecture is explained further in section tal.
    \item FMA2C~\cite{Ma2020}: an extension of the earlier MA2C method~\cite{Chu2019} that applies a feudal reinforcement learning architecture to hierarchically coordinate \gls{tsc} over multiple intersections. Each intersection is controlled by a local agent that learns independently, overseen by a regional manager that coordinates the local agents by establishing top-down goals.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Traditional \gls{tsc} methods and other baselines}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Stochastic: a method that randomly selects the next phase to be activated at each intersection. Not a traditional \gls{tsc} method in any sense, it is included as a baseline to provide a lower bound for the performance of other methods.
    \item Max Pressure: similar to the implementation of Chen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Chen2020} of the method devised by Varaiya~\cite{Varaiya2013}, it selects the next phase to be activated at each intersection based on the pressures for each phase, which is defined as the difference between the number of vehicles waiting at the incoming lanes associated with the phase and the number of vehicles waiting at the outgoing lanes.
    \item Max Wave: the \textit{Greedy} method defined by Chen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Chen2020}, it chooses phases based on the wave of vehicles approaching the intersection, which is calculated as the number of vehicles approaching on the lanes associated with each phase within a certain distance.
    \item Fixed time: a method that activates each phase for a fixed amount of time, regardless of traffic conditions. It was used as a compromise for the inability of obtaining optimal fixed times for all the intersections due to the difficulties laid out in Section~\ref{par:adios_fases}. Two different fixed times were tested, 20 and 40 seconds.
\end{itemize}

\section{Description of the proposed methods} \label{sec:proposed_models}
\subsection{Problem formulation} \label{subsec:meth_problem_form}
We modelled intermodal \acrfull{tsc} as a decentralized, partially
observable \acrfull{mdp}, which in this work we define by the octuple $\mathcal{M}=(S,A,T,R,\Omega,O,\gamma,I)$.

In the aforementioned tuple, $S,A,T,R$ correspond to the state, action, transition function and reward function definitions described in Section~\ref{subsubsec:mdp}; $\Omega$ denotes a finite set of observations where $o \in \Omega$; $O$ is an observation function defined as $O:S\times A \times \Omega \rightarrow [0,1]$; $\gamma$ represents the discount factor used in Q-learning —see Section~\ref{subsubsec:idqn}—; while $I$ corresponds to the set of signalized intersections in the scenario.

For both of the proposed models, each signalized intersection is controlled independently by an agent $i \in I$ that interacts with the traffic simulation deployed in SUMO via the \gls{traci} interface. At each time step $t$, agent $i$ receives a local observation $o_t^i=O(s_t)$ constructed from features of the lanes and pedestrian crossings it controls, selects a discrete action $a_t^i \in A^i$ corresponding to a predefined signal phase applied for a fixed duration, and subsequently receives a scalar reward $r_t^i$. The environment state evolves, then, according to the joint action of all agents and the underlying traffic dynamics simulated over a simulation step.

Each agent aims to optimize the \gls{tsc} probem through their reward function R. Their goal is to learn an optimal joint policy
\begin{equation} \label{eq:joint_policy}
    \pi( \bm{a}_t|\bm{s}_t)=\prod_{i \in I}\pi^i_\theta(a^i_t|o^i_t)
\end{equation}
maximizing the expected discounted return
\begin{equation} \label{eq:discount_return}
F(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i\in I}\gamma^{t}r_{t}^{i}\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\theta_i$ denotes the parameters of agent $i$'s policy and $\mathbb{E}$ refers to the averaged return over all possible trajectories across states and actions $\tau=(s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1,...)$ generated by following policy $\pi$ under the environment transition dynamics $T(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t)$. As the model definition is based on independent agents, the learnt policy is decentralized following $\pi=\{\pi_i \}_{i \in I}$, enabling each agent to independently maximize its own long-term cumulative reward.

The following sections treat in further detail, for each of the proposed methods, the definitions of the different parts of the described problem.

\subsection{The IDQN MM agent} \label{subsec:idqn_mm}

The first of the proposed agents is an extension of the IDQN agent developed by Ault \textit{et al.}~\cite{Ault2019} for a multimodal setting, which has been named as \textit{IDQN~MM}.

IDQN~MM instantiates one independent Q-learning agent per intersection according to the independent DQN (IDQN) architecture: each agent $i$ learns its own parametrized action-value function $Q(o_i, a_i; \theta_i)$, without explicit communication or parameter sharing with other agents.

\subsubsection {Action space} \label{subsubsec:idqn_action_space}

Each intersection $i$ exposes a discrete action space $a_t^i \in \{0, \dots, G_i - 1\}$, where $G_i$ is the number of predefined green phases at that intersection. Action spaces are, thus, heterogeneous across intersections. An action corresponds to a request for the signal to display the associated green phase.

\medskip

\noindent There are two circumstances in which action selection is not controlled by the agent:
 
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Automatic yellow phase insertion:} if the requested phase chosen by the agent differs from the currently active one, the environment does not switch directly; it inserts an automatically generated transitional yellow phase covering exactly the signal groups that move from green to red  between the two phases while not modifying the ones that were red, guaranteeing safe transitions between conflicting movements.

The length of this phase can be defined on a per-scenario basis. The chosen length used at the experiments performed in this work is listed in the relevant section,~\ref{subsubsec:params}.

\item \textbf{Anti-starvation cap:} A hyperparameter $H$ was added, setting a bound on how long the same green phase may be held across consecutive decisions. If the policy requests the same phase for $H$ consecutive control steps, the environment overrides the request and forces a switch to the next phase in round-robin order.

This hyperparameter was added after some baseline methods favoured excessively intersection movements with high traffic counts, especially in peak-hour demand scenarios. Similarly, Section~\ref{subsubsec:params} lists the value used in each scenario. 
\end{itemize}
 
\subsubsection{Multimodal state representation} \label{subsubsec:idqnmm_state}
 
The observation function produces, for each intersection $i$, a tensor $o_t^i$ containing the observations of all the lanes controlled by the intersection, which are described with a series features. As a result, the dimensions of this tensor are $1 \times L_i \times 9$, for $L_i$ is the number of lanes controlled by intersection $i$ and each lane $l \in L_i$ is described by a 9-dimensional feature vector.

This state representation is explained further in the following lines.

\medskip

\noindent \textbf{Range-limited sensing}
 
\noindent Entities are only considered detected on a lane if their distance to the next downstream traffic light along their route is within a fixed detection range, emulating a fixed-length detector zone rather. The value used in each scenario is reported in Section~\ref{subsubsec:params}.

Because SUMO's built-in per-vehicle waiting-time accumulator is not reliable across successive signals along a route, the agent maintains its own persistent per-vehicle waiting-time dictionary that is incremented locally at each step and cleared on departure.

\medskip

\noindent \textbf{Modal waiting time decomposition for vehicles}
 
\noindent Vehicles are classified as bicycles using their vehicle class or entity name. Then, per-lane waiting time is decomposed into two components: a bicycle-only component to be used in bike lanes, $\beta_t^l$, which is the sum of waiting time over detected bicycles; and a shared bicycle-car component for the rest of lanes, 
$\tau_{\text{car},t}^l = \max(0,\ \tau_t^l - \beta_t^l)$, where $\tau_l^t$ is the lane's total waiting time. This last subtraction  avoids double-counting waiting time in lanes with mixed car and bike traffic.
\medskip

\noindent \textbf{Per-lane feature vector}
 
\noindent For lane $l$ at intersection $i$, let $\mathcal{G}_t^i$ denote the set of lanes actively served (green) by the phase at time $t$ displayed at signal $i$, with this set being empty during yellow transitions.

\medskip

\noindent The feature vector is defined as
 
\begin{equation}
\bm{x}_{t}^{i,l} = \left(
\indicator [l \in \mathcal{G}_t^i],\;
\tau_{\text{car},t}^l,\;
\beta_t^l,\;
W_{\text{ped},t}^i\;
\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent where 
\begin{itemize}
    \item[] $\indicator [l \in \mathcal{G}_t^i]$ is used as an indicator function to mark whether lane $l$ pertains to the served lanes $\mathcal{G}_t^i$, in which case it equals 1, else 0;
    \item[] $\tau_{\text{car},t}^l$ and $\beta_t^l$ correspond to the modal waiting times defined some lineas earlier, normalized; and
    \item[] $W_{\text{ped},t}^i$ defines the normalized waiting time of pedestrians associated with the edge containing lane $l$, with its calculation being described more thoroughly in the next epigraph.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The observant reader will have noticed that the described feature vector only lists four and not nine features as the tensor description advanced. The missing features correspond to modal queue and speed values which are included for logging the method's performance; as these are not used by the IDQN~MM agent, they were omitted from this explanation for simplicity.

Those are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[]$n_{\text{appr}}^l$ is the count of approaching (i.e.\ not currently waiting) vehicles on the lane;
    \item[]$q_l^t$ and $q_l^{bike,t}$ are the (all-vehicle and bicycle-only) queue counts
    \item[]$\bar v_l^t$ is the mean speed of detected vehicles on the lane (zero if the lane is empty)
\end{itemize}
 
\noindent \textbf{Pedestrian sensing}

\noindent Pedestrian waiting times are computed at the intersection level rather than on a per-lane basis. First, the set of
edges controlled by intersection $i$ is obtained by mapping every controlled inbound/outbound lane to its parent edge. Over this edge set, the agent queries SUMO for all currently present pedestrians —using the \gls{traci} call \texttt{getLastStepPersonIDs}—, stores their IDs and, for each, retrieves its instantaneous waiting time. A pedestrian is counted as \textit{waiting} if this waiting time is  positive, including when it has physically left the edge to move to the nearby \texttt{walkingArea}; $W_{i,ped}^t$ is the sum of waiting time over waiting pedestrians, which resets when pedestrians have been served by a phase. As compound intersections can have successive pedestrian crossings, pedestrian IDs are kept and transferred across edges if these are still within the control of agent $i$.

Nevertheless, as shown in earlier lines, these pedestrian measures ultimately are included on all the lane feature vectors for the corresponding edge. This approach was chosen deliberately to align with the sensor approach that IDQN applies to cars, which would correspond to approach cameras in a pedestrian detection setting.
 
\subsubsection{Reward function} \label{subsubsec:idqnmm_reward}
 
The reward used by IDQN~MM is a scaled, clipped, negated delta of a weighted multimodal waiting-time cost.

Let the combined waiting-time cost at intersection $i$ and step $t$ be defined as

\begin{equation}
C_t^i = \sum_{l \in L_i} \tau_{\text{car},t}^l \;+\; w_{\text{bike}} \sum_{l \in L_i} \beta_t^l \;+\; w_{\text{ped}}\, W_{\text{ped},t}^i,
\end{equation}
 
\noindent with $w_{\text{bike}}$ and $w_{\text{ped}}$ being modal weight hyperparameters used to balance the reward signal across modes. The reward function is then
 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:idqnmm_reward}
r_t^i = \operatorname{clip}\!\left( -\frac{C_t^i - C_{t-1}^i}{\eta},\; -\kappa,\; \kappa \right),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\eta$ normalizes the cost delta and the $\pm \kappa$ clip  symmetrically bounds the reward magnitude. A positive reward therefore indicates that the intersection's combined multimodal waiting cost \emph{decreased} relative to the previous step.

Weights and constants are resolved on a per-scenario level. The ones used in the assessments performed in this work are listed in Section~\ref{subsec:experimental_setup}.

The choice for a delta-based reward function instead of the existing IDQN waiting time formulation was made during the development of the method, as the original function quickly became monotonic when agent performance was poor. Furthermore, the delta formulation makes the reward sensitive to local improvement or deterioration rather than to the absolute demand levels of each approach, which helps normalize reward scale across heterogeneously-loaded intersections. Clipping and normalization were added for increased optimization stability.
 
\subsubsection{Function approximation architecture} \label{subsubsec:idqnmm_architecture}

Instead of IDQN's original 2D convolution layer architecture, the proposed method relies on its own model architecture, based on a Deep Sets encoder \cite{Zaheer2017}, which has been named as \texttt{LaneWiseModel}.

\texttt{LaneWiseModel} processes each lane's feature vector independently, aggregating across them before action selection:
 
\begin{enumerate}
\item First, a shared-weight per-lane encoder $\phi_\theta$, a
two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) with rectifier linear unit (ReLU) activations, is applied identically to every lane's feature
vector $\bm{x}_t^{i,l}$, independent of lane identity or position.
\item The resulting per-lane embeddings are aggregated by mean pooling over the lane dimension,
$\bar h_t^i = \frac{1}{L_i}\sum_{l} \phi_\theta(\bm{x}_t^{i,l})$, yielding a fixed-size intersection embedding, independent of the  number of controlled lanes $L_i$ or their order.
\item A decision head composed of a further two-layer MLP followed by a PyTorch discrete action-value head maps
$\bar h_i^t$ to $Q(o_i^t, a,;\theta_i)$.
\end{enumerate}

While the original convolution layer architecture works on simpler intersections —as are the ones normally modelled in the current state of the art \cite{Ault2021}—, the complexity of some of the intersections in the present work scenarios is orders of magnitude larger. Pedestrian paths, bike or bus lanes, and especially compound intersections can result in disparate lane orderings that the existent model was not able to handle.

The chosen approach ensures permutation invariance to avoid this, as the same lane encoder is used on each lane and aggregated in an order-independent way, the resulting Q-function is invariant to the arbitrary ordering in which lanes are enumerated. This accommodates intersections with differing lane counts and topologies, especially when these are compound.
 
\subsubsection{Learning Algorithm} \label{subsubsec:idqn_learn_algo}
 
Each agent is trained with the standard off-policy deep Q-learning algorithm that IDQN used, with its objective being defined as
\begin{equation} \label{ec:idqnmm_obj}
L(\theta_i) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\big(r_t^i + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(o_{t+1}^i, a'; \theta_i^{-}) - Q(o_t^i, a_t^i; \theta_i)\big)^2\Big],
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\theta_i$ is the periodically-optimized target network and $\gamma$ the discount factor, which is used to determine the importance of future rewards compared to immediate ones.

Optimization is handled by adaptive moment estimation (Adam), with gradient-norm clipping; while exploration is $\varepsilon$-greedy with linear decay. For further training stability, the replay buffer is reset at the start of each training episode.

Honouring the independence claimed on its acronym, every intersection's network parameters, optimizer state, replay buffer, and exploration schedule are entirely separate; no gradient, parameter, or replay data is shared across intersections.

\subsection{The MPLight MM agent} \label{subsec:mplight_mm}
The second proposed method is an extension of the MPLight agent described by Chen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Chen2020}, adapted for a multimodal context. It receives the name of \textit{MPLight~MM}.

\medskip 

\noindent Whereas IDQN~MM instantiates one fully independent Q-learning agent per intersection, MPLight~MM is a \textit{shared-parameter} multi-agent formulation; as such, it is similar but also bears some differences.

Identically as IDQN~MM, every intersection, and thus every agent $i$, inputs its local state $o_t^i$ at time $t$ to a Q-network. However, while in the case of IDQN~MM this Q-network is a per-intersection copy, isolated and separate of the networks of the other intersections, for MPLight~MM the Q-network used is a single unified one, with identical weights across agents.
 
\subsubsection{Action space} \label{subsubsec:mp_action_space}
 
Due to the clashes between SUMO output limitations and the architecture of the original MPLight which will be explained in the following sections, the action space of MPLight~MM does not limit itself to a set of green phases, relying instead in a representation of demand groupings that allows the mapping between queues and traffic signal phases.

\medskip 

\noindent In MPLight~MM, all positions in controlled links that are given a green light in exactly the same subset of intersection $i$'s phases \textit{and} are not part of different sub-intersections are grouped together in a movement $m_i$. This yields, for each intersection $i$. a movement set $M_i =\{0,\dots,m_i-1\}$ along with inbound and outbound lane groupings associated to it, $L_i^{\text{in}}(m)$ and $L_i^{\text{out}}(m)$. This movement set $M_i$ is, in MPLight's original architecture, defined by a fixed template that does not vary its dimensions; in this proposed method, $M_i$ can adapt to intersections of differing geometries and signal plans.
 
For each green phase of intersection $i$, its two dominant served movements are identified and recorded as a phase pair $h = (m_a, m_b) \in M_i \times {M_i}$. Because MPLight~MM shares one Q-network with all intersections, all phase pairs produced this way across every intersection are concatenated into a single global, network-wide list $\Pi = (\varpi_0, \dots, \varpi_{N-1})$. As a result, the shared Q-network's action space can be defined as $\{0,\dots,N-1\}$, where $N$ corresponds the size of the phase pair list $\Pi$.

Each intersection $i$ only ever gets to select among its own subset of actions $\mathcal{N}_i \subseteq \{0,\dots,N-1\}$ of the action space, containing its own local phases, as $|\mathcal{N}_i| = G_i$. Hence, action selection and exploration are masked to $\mathcal{N}_i$ at decision time.
 
\subsubsection{Multimodal state representation} \label{subsubsec:mp_state}
 
To handle the monitoring of queues, the state function departs from IDQN~MM's per-lane representation and instead exposes, per intersection $i$, a fixed-width vector organized by movement and by global phase pair rather than by individual lane. 

For movement $m$, the queue pressures are computed following the inbound minus downstream pressure definition used in the original MPLight formulation, but classifying by mode and using the movement-derived lane mappings instead of a fixed direction template. This translates mathematically to
 
\begin{align}
\mathrm{car}_t^i(m) &= \sum_{l \in L_i^{\mathrm{in}}(m)} \big(q_{l,t} - q_{l,t}^{\mathrm{bike}}\big) \;- \sum_{l' \in L_i^{\mathrm{out}}(m)} \big(q_{l',t} - q_{l',t}^{\mathrm{bike}}\big), \text{ and}\\
\mathrm{bike}_t^i(m) &= \sum_{l \in L_i^{\mathrm{in}}(m)} q_{l,t}^{\mathrm{bike}} \;- \sum_{l' \in L_i^{\mathrm{out}}(m)} q_{l',t}^{\mathrm{bike}},
\end{align}
 
\noindent where $q_{l,t}$, $q_{l,t}^{\mathrm{bike}}$ are the queue counts on lane $l$ at
time $t$, for all vehicles and for bikes only, respectively. The subtraction performed to calculate car counts is done to handle lanes with mixed traffic without double counting. It is relevant to mention that this counting process is performed taking into account the detection distance and modal classification steps explained in IDQN MM's state representation, in Section~\ref{subsubsec:idqnmm_state}

\medskip 

\noindent Pedestrian demand, on the other hand, is not attached to a movement due to SUMO limitations.

Ideally, pedestrians waiting on the \texttt{waitingAreas} associated to a given crossing would get their counts used to calculate pressures for the relevant phase, as is done with vehicles. Unfortunately, SUMO does not offer a method to easily count the pedestrians waiting to cross a specific zebra crossing in a scalable way across different intersections. As was seen in the review of the state of the art in multimodal \gls{rltsc}, in Section~\ref{subsec:rlformtsc}, some works in the literature have tackled this problem with a whole pedestrian detection module overriding the simulator limitations \cite{Zhang2025}; however, these approaches greatly surpass the scope of this work.

As a reasonable replacement, pedestrian queues are collected with a method that SUMO does offer: \texttt{getServedPersonCount}. When inputted a SUMO phase index, this \gls{traci} call returns the number of waiting pedestrians that would be served by the given phase if it were green, and the difference in these values is what MPLight~MM uses as an equivalent to find the pedestrian pressure $p_t^i(n)$, and by proxy, all pedestrian queue metrics collected during the monitoring of other methods. As pedestrian crossings are not associated with phase pairs as vehicle movements are, $p_t^i(n)$ is directly attached to each global phase pair.

We are aware of the caveats that this avenue entails. Firstly, it is \textit{too perfect}: sensor setups for pedestrians are far away from perfect and it is not feasible to detect, at every moment, the exact number of pedestrians waiting for a specific traffic signal phase, especially when crossings are close to each other and thus people waiting for different phases share the same space. To add up to it, a more prosaic reason: repeated calls to the \texttt{getServedPersonCount} on large scenarios causes a segmentation fault in SUMO, and it was not possible to debug it during the development of MPLight~MM.

It was decided to adopt \texttt{getServedPersonCount} while not diminishing these warnings, accepting the fact that no pedestrian queues can be collected with it in larger scenarios and as a consequence, that the MPLight~MM method will not be assessed in those.

\medskip

\noindent Having stated these considerations, the full state vector can be defined finally as

\begin{equation}
o_t^i = \Big(\mathrm{phase}_t^i,\; \mathrm{car}_t^i(0), \mathrm{bike}_t^i(0),\; \dots,\; \mathrm{car}_t^i(M{-}1), \mathrm{bike}_t^i(M{-}1),\; p_t^i(0), \dots, p_t^i(N{-}1)\Big),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathrm{phase}_t^i$ is intersection $i$'s currently active local SUMO phase index; and $M$ corresponds to the largest movement count over the network following $M = \max_i M_i$, so that the other smaller movements can be zero-padded. In this way, the shared Q-learning network receives inputs of equal width across movements.
 
\subsubsection{Reward function} \label{subsubsec:mp_reward}

The reward function for MPLight~MM is chiefly a version of the one used in the original MPLight, an instantaneous, intersection-level, negated pressure, with a modal split and the addition of weights in a way analogous to IDQN~MM —which was presented in Section~\ref{subsubsec:idqnmm_reward}—:
 
\begin{equation}
r_t^i = -\Big( \mathrm{car}_t^i + w_{bike}\, \mathrm{bike}_t^i + w_{ped}\, \tilde p_t^i \Big),
\end{equation}
 
\noindent where $\tilde p_t^i$  is the total pedestrian pressure computed as explained in the previous section,  clipped \emph{before} weighting at a fixed ceiling hyperparameter $\bar P$ for enhanced stability. Akin to IDQN~MM, weights and other settings are resolved on a per-scenario level and compiled in the relevant section,~\ref{subsubsec:params}.
 
\subsubsection{Function approximation architecture}
 
The original MPLight relies for Q-learning approximation in the FRAP competition-graph architecture developed by Zheng \textit{et al.} specifically for \gls{rltsc}~\cite{Zheng2019}. This work proposes an extension of this architecture, modified for the handling of multimodal traffic, which has been given the name of \texttt{FRAP\_MM}.

The central inductive bias of FRAP is that the value of choosing a given phase pair depends on how its served movements' demand compares against \textit{every other} phase pair's demand, particularly those pairs that compete with it for a shared movement; the architecture therefore embeds every phase pair's demand and then explicitly scores every ordered pair of phase pairs together.
 
\medskip

\noindent First, for each movement $m \in \{0,\dots,M-1\}$, two elements are computed and concatenated into a per-movement representation $h_t^i(m)$: an indicator expressing whether $m$ is one of the two movements requested by the phase pair served at time $t$, and a demand embedding of that movement's car/bike pressure pair. This process is done via a sigmoid-activated phase embedding, a sigmoid-activated linear demand embedding, and a ReLU-activated linear layer combining the two; a structure defined already by the original FRAP demand embedding. The only difference that \texttt{FRAP\_MM} presents is its widening of the demand input, becoming 2-dimensional due to the modal split ($\mathrm{car}_t^i(m), \mathrm{bike}_t^i(m)$) rather than a single queue value.
 
\medskip

\noindent As was foretold, pedestrian pressure is injected at the phase-pair level. For pair $n = (m_a, m_b)$, the combined movement demand is formed as in MPLight's FRAP, while the pedestrian pressure for that same phase pair is mapped through a small linear embedding and added to form the combined representation
\begin{equation}
z_t^i(n) = h_t^i(m_a) + h_t^i(m_b) + \mathrm{ReLU}\big(p_t^{i\text{, emb}}(n)\big),
\end{equation}

\noindent All further steps of the model, mainly dealing with the competition graph component, are unmodified from the original FRAP architecture present in MPLight, so their exposition is deemed unnecessary.\footnote{The inquisitive reader can follow the internal processes of FRAP in the paper that describes it,~\cite{Zheng2019}.}
 
\subsubsection{Learning algorithm}
\label{subsubsec:mplight-learning}
 
Training follows the same off-policy DQN structure described for IDQN~MM in Section~\ref{subsubsec:idqn_learn_algo} (target network, replay buffer, Adam, gradient clipping, $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration), but with several changes reflecting its shared-parameter conception. For they stem mainly from MPLight's original architecture, its listing in this work is summarized. For more details about it, the reader can look up the original paper by Chen \textit{et al.}~\cite{Chen2020}.

\medskip

\noindent \textbf{Shared DQN architecture}

\noindent All $i \in I$ intersections' local states are packed into a single batch at every control step and passed through the single shared, instead of the fully independent procedure that IDQN~MM follows.

\medskip

\noindent \textbf{Action masking}

\noindent As each intersection $i$ can only execute its own local phases, greedy action selection and $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration are limited to $i$'s own subset of actions $\mathcal{N}_i$ rather than the full global one $\{0,\dots,N-1\}$.

\medskip

\noindent \textbf{Exploration and update scalability}

\noindent The linear $\varepsilon$-decay schedule is stretched by the number of intersections, the target-network update interval is scaled by the number of agents and the optimizer update interval is set to the agent number. This measure keeps exploration and update intervals in a reasonable ratio in respect to the data.
